## Abstract

### Background

Lysine pyruvylation (Kpy) is a lysine post-translational modification reported in 2026, catalysed by HAT1/p300 and erased by SIRT3, whose substrate is pyruvate — the terminal product of glycolysis. Two 2026 studies showed that Kpy suppresses type-I interferon (IFN-I) signalling via STAT1 pyruvylation and couples glycolytic flux to epigenetic transcription. Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) combines hyperglycaemia, hypoxia, high glycolytic flux and recurrent infection; KpyRN has not been examined in DFU.

### Methods

We constructed a 128-gene, five-module, fully source-traceable KpyRN gene set from the two primary Kpy publications plus curated pathway resources, and evaluated it across ten public human DFU-related datasets: five bulk cohorts, two single-cell cohorts, one spatially resolved cohort, one longitudinal infection cohort and one independent outcome cohort; seven of these contributed to computation. Differential expression was assessed per cohort with limma (3.62.2) and combined by random-effects meta-analysis (metafor 5.2.1, REML); because each meta-analysis combines only two cohorts, cohort-specific direction concordance rather than nominal pooled p values provides the primary corroboration. Weighted module–trait relationships (WGCNA 1.74) and a four-algorithm consensus machine-learning framework (LASSO, random forest, SVM-RFE, XGBoost; 10 pre-specified seeds) were applied to a 26-gene candidate pool, with feature selection performed inside each cross-validation fold for the per-algorithm estimates; the consensus gene set and the consensus logistic-regression model were selected and fitted on the full training set and are therefore evaluated only in external cohorts, with no internal performance estimate. Immune infiltration was estimated with three independent deconvolution algorithms (EPIC, MCP-counter, ESTIMATE). Causal direction for glycaemic traits was probed by cis-expression quantitative trait locus (cis-eQTL) Mendelian randomisation. Therapeutic tractability was assessed by AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 docking against five Kpy-pathway proteins with positive and negative controls in eight receptor–ligand systems; a tissue RT-qPCR protocol with sample size derived from the observed pooled effect sizes was specified at the design level only and was **not executed**.

### Results

Across two DFU cohorts, META1 tested 11,615 genes and returned 2,863 meta-DEGs at FDR < 0.05. Of the 97 scored KpyRN genes represented in that meta-analysis, 26 were significant (26.8%, versus 24.6% of non-KpyRN genes; Fisher exact OR 1.12, p = 0.64, i.e. no module-level enrichment), and **all 26 showed concordant direction between cohorts**. Thirteen glycolytic enzymes were up-regulated (PGAM1 +1.25 log2 fold change (log2FC), padj = 1.5 × 10⁻³²; TPI1 +1.29; GAPDH +1.32; LDHA +1.00; PFKP +0.90; SLC16A1 +0.83), whereas the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier was reduced (MPC2 −0.45). Five IFN-I read-out transcripts were down-regulated (IFI44L −1.09; IFI27 −1.02; DHX58 −0.74; DDX58 −0.47; STAT2 −0.41) while ISG20 and GBP1 were up-regulated, indicating heterogeneity within the interferon-stimulated gene class rather than uniform suppression. The three-layer intersection of meta-DEG, WGCNA module membership and KpyRN comprised six glycolytic enzymes (BPGM, LDHA, PFKP, PGAM1, PGM2, TPI1), but a pre-specified independence test attributed it entirely to the pyruvate-supply module: excluding that module left no gene at all, and the number of KpyRN genes entering the DFU-correlated pool did not exceed random expectation (6 observed versus 8.9 expected, p = 0.19). The IFN-I module was under-represented in that pool (0 of 21 background-covered genes, expected 3.3; one-sided p = 0.026, two-sided p = 0.052, BH q = 0.18 across seven subsets), which we report as a directional observation and not as a significant result. A six-gene consensus logistic-regression classifier (ADH1B, ALDH3A2, DHX58, FAHD2B, GBP1, LDHA) separated DFU from control completely in GSE199939 (area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve [AUC] 1.000; the exact probability of perfect separation under the null is 1/C(21,10) = 2.8 × 10⁻⁶), but in two pre-specified forearm-skin contrasts it scored 0.05 (n = 9; significantly below chance, exact one-sided p = 0.016) and 0.69 (n = 8), and it showed no cross-validated signal for healing outcome. Immune deconvolution did not converge on a single phenotype: GSE134431 showed reduced cytotoxic T-cell, NK and myeloid-dendritic-cell signatures (six metrics, all FDR < 0.05), whereas in GSE199939 the significant changes were *increased* monocytic-lineage, CD4 T-cell, neutrophil and fibroblast scores with decreased myeloid-DC scores, and no cytotoxic or NK metric was significant (FDR 0.30–0.79). Single-cell pseudobulk in GSE165816 confirmed coordinate glycolytic up-regulation in keratinocytes. Two further public cohorts were not evaluable at the inferential level — GSE223964 (five versus three donors) and GSE166120 (two versus two subjects) cannot produce a significant donor- or subject-level result at any observed effect size — and a longitudinal *S. aureus* infection cohort (GSE230426) showed a significant decline of M4 IFN-I and M3 Kpy-target module scores in PBMC during infection resolution, with all six hub genes negative. Mendelian randomisation identified one FDR-significant causal estimate, DHX58 → type 2 diabetes (Wald β = −0.0712, SE 0.0195; 95% CI −0.109 to −0.033; p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴, FDR = 0.0088; OR = 0.931, 95% CI 0.896–0.968). Docking protocol validation passed in all eight systems (positive-control re-docking RMSD < 0.001 Å; the controls are rigid, so this validates box placement and receptor preparation rather than the flexible torsional search) and 29 unique candidates were docked against five target proteins; no candidate outperformed its crystallographic cofactor in any system except one shallow, non-discriminating pocket.

### Conclusions

The transcriptomic footprint of the Kpy regulatory network is **altered but directionally heterogeneous** in diabetic foot ulcer. The reproducible signal is a tissue-resident shift towards glycolytic dominance with selective — not global — attenuation of IFN-I read-out transcripts. Evidence for KpyRN alteration is strongest in bulk transcriptome meta-analysis and in the module layer of an independent PBMC infection cohort; in two further cohorts the donor- and subject-level contrasts were not evaluable at any attainable effect size. The dissociation between a glycolytic axis that is coupled to the DFU co-expression network and an IFN-I axis that appears uncoupled from it is compatible with — but does not establish — two partially independent transcriptional programs. We did not measure Kpy itself; no wet-lab validation was performed and none is planned, and a pre-specified tissue RT-qPCR protocol (n = 10 per group) is provided as an unexecuted design document. These data support the Kpy transcriptional footprint as a candidate readout of the DFU metabolic state. The boundary of that support is set by the limitations listed above.

## 1. Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is a common and expensive complication of diabetes. In China, the reported incidence of diabetic foot is 8.1%, the lifetime risk of developing foot lesions reaches 34%, and among patients with diabetic foot the overall amputation rate is 19.03% (2.14% major and 16.88% minor) and the annual mortality rate is 14.4% [1]. Infection is the leading cause of prolonged non-healing and amputation; healing rates at 12 weeks of treatment range from 24% to 82%, and the recurrence rate within one year after healing is 31.6% in individuals older than 50 [1]. Mechanistically, the DFU wound bed is characterised by hyperglycaemia, hypoxia, high glycolytic flux and repeated bacterial infection, and by an impaired local immune response.

Lysine pyruvylation (Kpy) is a post-translational modification of lysine residues whose defining feature is that its substrate is pyruvate, the terminal product of glycolysis. Two studies published in 2026 established the molecular machinery of this modification. Zuo and colleagues showed in *Cell* that elevated glycolytic flux supplies pyruvate for STAT1 pyruvylation at K201, which impairs STAT1–STAT2 dimerisation, attenuates type-I interferon signalling and suppresses antibacterial and antiviral immunity; in patients with hyperglycaemia, STAT1 pyruvylation in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was elevated and interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression reduced (r ≈ −0.75 to −0.82) [2]. Song and colleagues showed in *Nature Metabolism* that Kpy is enzymatically reversible — written by HAT1 and p300 using pyruvoyl-CoA, erased by SIRT3 — and that Kpy localises to promoters, activating genes including FGFR4, LGALS8 and RSPH4A; hypoxia, respiratory-chain inhibition and pyruvate supplementation all increase Kpy [3]. A later review listed diabetes and cardiometabolic disease among the unexplored directions for Kpy research [4].

Neither the Kpy regulatory network (KpyRN) nor any other post-translational-modification network has been examined in diabetic complications or in the DFU transcriptome. The omission matters because the Kpy machinery is specified well enough for a network-level transcriptomic question to be testable: a writer–eraser pair (HAT1/p300 and SIRT3) acts on pyruvate, a substrate whose supply glycolysis sets. Results can therefore be read against that mechanism rather than treated as an agnostic gene list. The downstream consequence reported in the source studies — suppression of IFN-I-mediated antimicrobial immunity — is also the process implicated in what makes DFU fail: infection, non-healing, amputation. The nearest competing axis in DFU bioinformatics is lysine lactylation, also driven by glycolytic intermediates but without a writer–eraser pair and without a direct antiviral-immune read-out.

This study asks four questions: (Q1) Is the KpyRN dysregulated in DFU ulcer tissue relative to non-ulcer and healthy skin, and does the direction of dysregulation match that predicted by increased pyruvate availability? (Q2) Does a stable subset of KpyRN genes discriminate DFU from control and stratify healing outcome? (Q3) Are KpyRN abnormalities coupled to immune-infiltrate remodelling and to specific cell types? (Q4) Is there causal evidence for a KpyRN gene influencing glycaemic traits, and are Kpy-pathway proteins tractable by small molecules?

We pre-specified falsification conditions: if the KpyRN meta-analytic signal were no stronger than the genome-wide background rate, if hub genes failed to reproduce in an external cohort, or if a validation cohort were negative, we would report the negative result and reframe the KpyRN as a readout of the DFU metabolic state rather than as a mechanism. All three cohorts are reported as analysed, including the negative results.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Study design, data sources and ethics

The study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, de-identified human transcriptomic data. No new human participants were enrolled and no identifiable private information was accessed; no attempt at re-identification was made, and the original studies' ethical approvals were obtained by their respective submitters. Accordingly, institutional review board approval was not applicable to the analyses reported here, and no new IRB review was sought or obtained. The pre-specified wet-lab validation protocol (Section 2.12) is a design document only: it was not executed and no execution is planned, and the ethics pathway that would be required is specified in Supplementary Material S4 for the benefit of groups intending to run it. Reporting followed the STROBE statement for observational cohort analyses, STROBE-MR for the Mendelian randomisation component, TRIPOD for the prediction model, and the MIQE guidelines for the pre-specified RT-qPCR protocol.

Ten GEO series were retrieved (Supplementary Table S2). Five bulk cohorts (GSE134431 [5], GSE199939 [6,36], GSE143735 [7], GSE80178 [8], GSE68183/GSE68186 [9,10]), two single-cell cohorts (GSE165816 [11], GSE223964 [15]), one spatially resolved cohort (GSE166120 [13]) and one longitudinal infection cohort (GSE230426 [14,16]); of these, GSE80178 and GSE68183/GSE68186 could not be processed at sample level and were not used computationally (Section 4.7, item 2). Sample-level metadata for all 14 inventoried GEO series (283 samples; Supplementary Table S2) were retrieved with NCBI E-utilities and are archived with SHA-256 checksums in the project MANIFEST.

We corrected two entries in the original data inventory. First, GSE166120 was initially catalogued as a Visium spatial transcriptomics series; inspection of the GEO series matrix and the primary publication (Theocharidis et al., *Nature Communications*, 2022 [12]; GEO series title: *Spatial transcriptomics of healing and non-healing diabetic foot ulcers*) established that it is a **NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) experiment** using the Cancer Transcriptome Atlas (CTA) v2.0 targeted panel (1,825 targets), in which each GSM corresponds to a single region of interest (ROI) rather than a spatial spot. Second, GSE165816 gene identifiers were confirmed at the file level to be gene symbols with zero Ensembl-prefixed identifiers, making the identifier-mapping step a verified no-op rather than an assumption.

### 2.2 Construction of the KpyRN gene set

We assembled a five-module gene set and traced every entry to a named source (Supplementary Table S1; full table in `01_数据/基因集/KpyRN_modules.csv`).

- **M1 — Kpy machinery (writer/eraser/substrate; 4 genes):** HAT1, EP300, SIRT3, STAT1, from *Nature Metabolism* 2026 [3] and *Cell* 2026 [2].
- **M2 — pyruvate supply and diversion (90 genes):** the core glycolytic enzyme set (HK1/HK2/GCK, GPI, PFKM/PFKL/PFKP, ALDOA, TPI1, GAPDH, PGK1, PGAM1, ENO1/ENO2, PKM, LDHA/LDHB, PGAM2, PGAM5, PGM2, BPGM, ALDOA, etc.), mitochondrial pyruvate import and oxidation (SLC16A1, MPC1, MPC2, PDHA1, PDK1–PDK4, PDHB, PC, ALDH4A1), drawn from KEGG hsa00010 and hsa00620 and from the pyruvate-supply arm of [3].
- **M3 — experimentally validated Kpy targets (3 genes):** FGFR4, LGALS8, RSPH4A, identified by CUT&Tag and RNA-seq in [3].
- **M4 — IFN-I read-out (31 genes):** IFNAR1/2, JAK1, TYK2, STAT2, IRF9, IRF1 and canonical ISGs (IFIT1/2/3, MX1/2, OAS1/2/3/L, ISG15, ISG20, IFI6/16/27/44/44L, IFITM1/3, RSAD2, DDX58, DHX58, EIF2AK2, CXCL10, GBP1), from [2] plus KEGG hsa04064 and Reactome JAK-STAT signalling.
- **M5 — histone substrate carriers (12 genes):** histone genes, retained for provenance but **excluded from all scoring** because histone modification is cell-cycle dependent and would confound module scores in proliferating wound-edge tissue.

The scored set therefore contains **128 genes**; M5 is reported only in the provenance table.

### 2.3 Preprocessing, differential expression and random-effects meta-analysis

Each cohort was processed independently; matrices were never concatenated across platforms, to avoid platform-composition artefacts. GSE134431 was provided as RPKM, GSE199939 as TPM and GSE143735 as raw counts; all were log2-transformed, and gene symbols were harmonised by HGNC mapping with a documented conflict rule (maximum mean expression retained). Low-expressed genes were filtered within cohort. GSE165816 was processed per sample with filters of 200 ≤ n_genes ≤ 8,000 and pct_mt ≤ 20; 174,962 raw cells yielded 167,837 passing cells (95.93%).

Differential expression was performed per cohort with limma (3.62.2; R 4.4.3) [18, 25]. Effect sizes were then combined across cohorts by random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum likelihood in `metafor` (5.2.1) [19], retaining I² as a heterogeneity measure and providing cohort-specific log2FC and standard errors for every gene. Because each meta-analysis combines only two cohorts, τ² is estimated from a single degree of freedom and the default Wald z test is anti-conservative; we therefore report cohort-specific effect directions and their concordance as the primary corroboration of each pooled estimate, and interpret the pooled p values as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. A Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustment is not applied, because at k = 2 its t₁ reference distribution cannot reject any plausible null and would vacate all findings, so direction concordance and per-cohort Benjamini–Hochberg significance carry the inferential weight. Prediction intervals are not reported, because they require k ≥ 3, and where I² exceeds 75% the cohort-specific estimates rather than the pooled value are the interpretable quantity. Two meta-analyses were specified in advance: **META1**, DFU versus non-ulcer/control skin in GSE134431 and GSE199939 (two cohorts); **META2**, healer versus non-healer in GSE134431 and GSE143735. Per-cohort DEG counts were: GSE134431 DFU vs diabetic foot skin 2,814/12,450 tested (817 up, 1,997 down); GSE199939 DFU vs control 9,773/17,239 tested (3,958 up, 5,815 down), consistent with the 8,800 DEG reported in the primary publication of that cohort [6]. The three pre-specified outcome contrasts yielded zero DEG at FDR < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg [17]) (GSE134431 healer vs non-healer; GSE143735 healer vs non-healer; GSE143735 DFU vs diabetic non-ulcer).

### 2.4 Weighted module–trait analysis and the three-layer intersection

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis was run on GSE134431 (top 5,000 expressed genes) [20]. The soft-thresholding power was set to 6 as a fallback because the soft-threshold fit was unstable at n = 21; this is recorded as a limitation, and module results were used only as an intersection layer, never as an independent biological claim. Ten modules were detected (nine non-grey), of which four correlated significantly with DFU status. The candidate pool for machine learning was defined as the union of the two-layer intersection (meta-DEG ∩ KpyRN) and the three-layer intersection (meta-DEG ∩ DFU-correlated WGCNA module ∩ KpyRN), giving 26 genes.

### 2.5 Consensus machine learning

Four algorithms (LASSO with 10-fold cross-validated penalty [22], random forest [21], SVM-RFE [24], XGBoost [23]) were run in parallel over 10 pre-specified random seeds on the discovery set (GSE134431) with two tasks: Task A, DFU versus diabetic foot skin (diagnosis); Task B, healer versus non-healer (outcome). A gene was designated a consensus hub gene if selected by at least three of the four algorithms. For each algorithm, feature selection was performed inside every cross-validation fold, so the per-algorithm out-of-fold AUCs are leakage-free; the consensus gene set and the consensus logistic-regression model were, by contrast, selected and fitted on the full training set and are therefore evaluated only in external cohorts, with no internal performance estimate. Performance was assessed by repeated five-fold cross-validation in the training set and by external validation in GSE199939 (Task A) and GSE143735 (Task A and Task B), reporting AUC with DeLong confidence intervals; because the ten seeds re-partition the same 21 samples, the reported standard deviations describe cross-validation variability and are not standard errors of the true performance. Hyperparameters were fixed in advance (random forest 500 trees; XGBoost 200 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.05; LASSO penalty chosen by nested three-fold cross-validation) and are recorded with the seeds (1–10) in `02_脚本/ml_biomarker.py`. All six consensus genes were present in both external cohorts, so no external evaluation was skipped; an absent gene would have been recorded as SKIPPED rather than imputed. No calibration statistic is reported, because a six-predictor logistic model fitted on 21 samples is over-fitted by construction.

### 2.6 Immune infiltration deconvolution

The `immunedeconv` Bioconductor package has been withdrawn and `xCell2` requires R ≥ 4.5, neither available in our environment (R 4.4.3 / Bioconductor 3.20). We therefore implemented three deconvolution algorithms from their published signatures and source code: **EPIC** (6 BRef non-malignant reference populations, NNLS with a unit-sum constraint) [26], **MCP-counter** (10 populations from the official 111-gene signature, implemented from the ebecht/MCPcounter source) [27], and **ESTIMATE** (140 stromal + 140 immune genes). Groups were compared by Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction [17], and hub-gene and KpyRN-module scores were correlated with immune estimates by Spearman rank correlation with FDR correction. The MCP-counter signature and the ESTIMATE signature matrix are archived under `01_数据/基因集/免疫/`. The EPIC reference matrix ships inside the `GfellerLab/EPIC` R package (v1.1.7) and is not duplicated as a flat file; the calling script (`02_脚本/immune_deconv.R`) and its outputs (`03_结果/免疫浸润/*_epic_fractions.csv`) are archived.

### 2.7 Single-cell analysis

For GSE165816 (54 samples), cells were annotated by a marker-score procedure (argmax of mean expression of 23 lineage marker sets on CP10K + log1p scale); cells whose top-two marker scores differed by less than 0.25 were labelled ambiguous (12.7%). Sanity checks against expected tissue composition passed (PBMC 84.7% haematopoietic, foot skin 73.4%, forearm skin 79.8% stromal/parenchymal). KpyRN module and hub-gene scores were computed per donor × cell type from pseudobulk counts per million (CPM, log2), restricted to donor × lineage combinations with ≥ 100 cells, and compared at donor level by Wilcoxon rank-sum test with BH correction. This is a marker-scoring annotation rather than reference-transcriptome projection (SingleR/Azimuth were not used), and sub-lineage conclusions were treated as exploratory.

For GSE223964, a processed *h5ad* (27,998 cells × 18,287 genes; X already log1p-scaled with no raw counts), pseudobulk was computed as the per-donor, per-cell-type mean of log1p values (no counts summation), and module scores were compared between diabetic (n = 5 donors) and non-diabetic (n = 3 donors) skin by Mann–Whitney U (exact). A second donor-level path (median pseudobulk, 10,000 label permutations) was implemented in a separate script to guard against a computation defect masquerading as a negative result; it uses the same eight donors and the same cells, so it tests the computation and not the biology. With five versus three donors the smallest attainable two-sided exact p is 2/C(8,3) = 0.036, and after correction across 32 contrasts the smallest attainable q is 1.0; this cohort is therefore treated as non-evaluable rather than negative.

### 2.8 Spatially resolved analysis (GSE166120, GeoMx DSP)

Spatially resolved profiling was used to ask whether the KpyRN signal varies between anatomical regions of the same ulcer, in the sense of regional composition rather than whole-transcriptome spatial mapping [28]. GeoMx DSP DCC files were parsed at probe level and mapped to genes via the CTA v2.0 PKC `SystematicName` field, taking the first non-empty systematic name per probe. We rejected `DisplayName` because 24 targets mix probes from multiple genes. Taking only the first element of `SystematicName` also fails, because some probes carry an empty list and the index mis-aligns. This mapped 8,670/8,670 probes and 1,825/1,825 targets with zero omissions. The 88 ERCC negative-control probes were excluded. Because DCC files provide probe-level read counts without upstream DSQ2 QC, no GC/area/nucleus normalisation was applied and only relative abundance (composition) across ROIs was compared; absolute quantification is not claimed. Module scores were computed by standardising each gene across ROIs (not each ROI across genes — the latter is mathematically zero by construction) and then averaging across genes. The primary inferential unit was the **subject** (n = 4; ROIs are nested within subject); ROI-level tests (n = 23) are reported as sensitivity analyses only.

### 2.9 Longitudinal infection-cohort analysis (GSE230426)

GSE230426 is a longitudinal PBMC cohort of patients with *S. aureus* diabetic foot infection [14] (20 baseline and 18 post-treatment samples, 15 at 8 weeks and 3 at 4 weeks). The public metadata do not provide healing outcome, so a healed-versus-non-healed contrast was not constructed. Because no explicit donor identifier is present, pairing was inferred from contiguous sample identifiers with matching age and complementary time points (17 pairs, 4 singletons); the **primary model was therefore an unpaired limma-voom analysis**, with inferred pairing and an after-8-week-only model as sensitivity analyses. Module-level testing used `camera()` competitive gene-set testing over the 128 scored KpyRN genes.

### 2.10 Mendelian randomisation

Following a pre-registered feasibility assessment, cis-eQTL instruments for hub genes were taken from eQTLGen lead SNPs and used in two-sample MR [29] against type 2 diabetes (two sources) and fasting insulin, with the Wald ratio for a single instrument; alleles were harmonised and every exclusion logged. Instrument strength was assessed by F statistic, with F > 10 required for inclusion. **Limitations stated in advance.** With one cis-eQTL instrument per gene the estimate is a Wald ratio and the instrumental-variable assumptions cannot be tested: MR-Egger, weighted median, Cochran's Q and leave-one-out analyses are undefined, and no Steiger directionality test was performed. Replication in a second outcome GWAS is therefore the only available sensitivity check. Sample overlap between eQTLGen (up to 31,684 whole-blood donors) and the T2D consortia cannot be excluded and would bias estimates towards the observational association, and because the lead eQTL was selected for significance in the same eQTLGen dataset, winner's curse may attenuate the exposure beta. Thirty-four of the 39 possible gene–outcome pairs were analysable; five were absent from the outcome GWAS and are logged in Supplementary Table S8a.

### 2.11 Molecular docking

AutoDock Vina 1.2.7 was used with exhaustiveness 8 and seed 42 [30, 31]. Receptors were human experimental structures (PKM2 4JPG, EP300 4PZS, SIRT3 4BN4, LDHA 4JNK, PDK2 4MPC/2BU8/2BU5), protonated and converted to PDBQT with Open Babel [32] after stripping flexible-residue tags; the docking box was centred on the centroid of the crystallographic ligand. Ligands were prepared with meeko/RDKit from ChEMBL records ranked by pChEMBL ≥ 5 plus literature-verified compounds. **Protocol validation:** in every one of the eight receptor–ligand systems the crystallographic ligand was re-docked and heavy-atom RMSD computed (Kabsch, MODEL 1 only, index-paired); the acceptance criterion was RMSD < 2 Å. Positive controls were prepared with Open Babel and are rigid (zero rotatable bonds), whereas candidates were prepared with meeko/RDKit and are flexible (11–15 rotatable bonds); this asymmetry is disclosed because it biases Δ in favour of the rigid reference, which carries no intramolecular strain term. Affinities are reported relative to the co-crystallographic ligand in the same pocket (Δ = candidate − co-crystal), and, because Vina scores improve with molecular size, ligand efficiency (affinity per heavy atom) is reported alongside in Supplementary Table S9c. Each system was run once (exhaustiveness 8, seed 42), so no run-to-run variance is available and Vina's typical scoring error of 2–3 kcal/mol applies to every Δ. Sucrose was docked in every system as a negative control. We use docking only to generate hypotheses; these affinities are not binding evidence.

### 2.12 A pre-specified, unexecuted tissue RT-qPCR protocol (design record)

A tissue-level RT-qPCR protocol was pre-specified in full but is **not executed, and no execution is planned**; it is retained only as a design record, contributes no data to this paper, and carries no evidential weight for any claim. The original design included six consensus hub genes and six candidate reference genes, with sample size derived from the **observed** pooled effects in META1 (n = 10 per group, 12 recruited to allow 20% attrition); it was later reduced at the design level to two axis-representative targets — LDHA (glycolytic arm) and DHX58 (IFN-I arm) — plus two references to be chosen post hoc from HPRT1, RPLP0 and TBP, and restated as four directed hypotheses (Section 3.2 and Supplementary Material S4b). That reduction followed from two findings. First, the independence test reframed KpyRN as two partially independent axes rather than one co-regulated module — an interpretation we treat as a hypothesis, not a finding (Section 4.1). Second, the final BLAST pass disqualified six of the twelve assays — the three remaining metabolic targets (FAHD2B, ADH1B, ALDH3A2) and three of the six reference candidates (ACTB, GAPDH, SDHA) — through perfect or near-perfect 3'-end off-target matches that no re-design can remove (class-I alcohol-dehydrogenase and actin-family paralogs for ADH1B and ACTB; uncharacterized LOC loci for FAHD2B, GAPDH and SDHA; 210 near-perfect hits for the ALDH3A2 reverse primer). That the two canonical housekeeping genes failed is itself informative: reference genes cannot be adopted from the literature without per-primer testing. Primer sequences (designed with primer3 [33]), Tm and GC windows, secondary-structure checks, BLAST specificity tables and the LDHA 3'UTR anchoring required by the processed pseudogene LOC105375980 are documented in Supplementary Materials S4 and Table S10, reported against the Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments (MIQE) guidelines [34] with reference-gene selection by geNorm [35], and are not repeated here, while the LDHA assay's own limitation is stated in Section 4.7, item 9.

### 2.13 Statistical principles, reproducibility and audit

Every number in this manuscript is registered in a machine-readable audit ledger (`05_稿件/audit_facts.json`) that records, for each value, the source file and the extraction expression used; a verification script re-derives every manuscript figure from the ledger, so no expected value is hard-coded. This discipline exposed five classes of upstream defect, reported transparently in Section 2.14. Analyses used fixed seeds; all software versions (including R 4.4.3 [25]) and file checksums are recorded.

### 2.14 Defects found and fixed during analysis

Five classes of upstream defect were detected and fixed during this study; they are recorded because they affect how the numbers reported below should be read. (i) A PDBQT parser summed atoms across all VINA MODEL blocks. Receptors whose output contained 2 or 9 models (1OX, TF1, PV2, TF4) failed index pairing, and the positive-control RMSD was missing for four systems — including all three PDK2 sites. Four single-model systems passed fortuitously and masked the defect. Restricting parsing to MODEL 1 recomputed all eight systems below 0.001 Å. (ii) A figure routine applied a Kabsch superposition across two different molecules with unequal atom counts, which is dimensionally invalid; poses were re-rendered as direct 2-D projections in the receptor frame and captioned as such. (iii) In the single-cell cohort GSE223964, the grouping variable was categorical and pandas' default `observed=False` expanded it into a 16-cell Cartesian product containing 8 empty groups, producing NaN means, spurious group counts (8 versus 8) and entirely empty p-value columns; with `observed=True` the true donor counts (5 versus 3) and valid statistics were recovered. The same analysis had used a paired Wilcoxon test for two independent donor groups, corrected to Mann–Whitney U (exact) with the downstream column references updated in the same commit. (iv) A z-score computed across genes within each ROI is mathematically zero by construction (the row mean of a z-scored row is zero); the reported magnitudes of ~10⁻¹⁷ were misread as a successful run. Standardisation was moved to the gene-across-ROI direction, verified by hand-recomputation of one ROI–gene pair to 1.011253. In the GeoMx series, repeated `!Sample_characteristics_ch1` lines with identical labels were being collapsed by a dictionary keyed on the label (silently discarding fields), the GSM identifier was being read from the title row rather than the accession row, and `Non-ulcer`/`Non-Ulcer` spelling variants were splitting one anatomical stratum into two. Finally, the inventory itself was wrong in one respect: GSE166120 was catalogued as Visium and is in fact GeoMx DSP, which changes the evidential weight of that cohort from spatial transcriptomics to partial-coverage targeted profiling. (v) The BLAST specificity pipeline (verify_primers_blast_v6.py) returned an empty hit list whenever the NCBI search status was not READY, and this was misread as zero off-targets, so several primers (FAHD2B-F, ADH1B-F, ALDH3A2-R, ACTB-R) were wrongly reported as NO_TARGET / target-transcript hits = 0. The pipeline was replaced by regen_ldha_v5_blast.py, which retries up to three times and marks INCONCLUSIVE rather than reporting absence of evidence as evidence of absence; all 12 pairs were re-checked from a single authoritative BLAST pass and the specificity counts in the audit ledger were regenerated accordingly.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The KpyRN expression signature in DFU tissue: concordant direction but no module-level enrichment

META1 (DFU versus non-ulcer skin, two cohorts, REML random effects) tested 11,615 genes and returned 2,863 meta-DEGs at FDR < 0.05. Of the 128 scored KpyRN genes, 26 were significant, and **all 26 showed concordant direction across the two cohorts** (26/26). The signal was dominated by two oppositely signed modules: thirteen glycolytic enzymes were up-regulated (M2), and five IFN-I read-out transcripts were down-regulated (M4). Representative glycolytic effects were PGAM1 +1.25 log2FC (padj = 1.5 × 10⁻³²), TPI1 +1.29 (1.4 × 10⁻⁶), GAPDH +1.32 (1.0 × 10⁻²), PGK1 +1.07 (1.4 × 10⁻¹⁵), LDHA +1.00 (1.1 × 10⁻⁹), PFKP +0.90 (3.3 × 10⁻⁷), SLC16A1 +0.83 (2.4 × 10⁻⁴), LDHB +0.75, PGM2 +0.67 (2.4 × 10⁻³), ALDOA +0.66, ENO1 +1.24 (3.2 × 10⁻²), BPGM +0.83 and PDHB +0.26. Within the IFN-I read-out, IFI44L was reduced by −1.09 (1.3 × 10⁻⁶), IFI27 −1.02 (1.8 × 10⁻⁶), DHX58 −0.74 (8.6 × 10⁻⁵), DDX58 −0.47 (9.1 × 10⁻³) and STAT2 −0.41 (3.5 × 10⁻³), while ISG20 (+0.88, 3.9 × 10⁻³) and GBP1 (+0.84, 1.3 × 10⁻³) were increased, and MPC2 — the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier — was reduced (−0.45, 3.0 × 10⁻³), together with ADH1B (−4.42), ALDH3A2 (−1.63) and FAHD2B (−1.12). These last three carry an explicit heterogeneity warning: their cohort-specific estimates were −2.79 (SE 0.44) versus −6.29 (SE 1.00) for ADH1B (I² = 90.2%), −1.00 (SE 0.28) versus −2.24 (SE 0.23) for ALDH3A2 (I² = 91.2%) and −0.87 (SE 0.12) versus −1.38 (SE 0.14) for FAHD2B (I² = 87.1%), so their pooled values average two materially different cohort effects and should not be read as a single common effect. Across all 11,615 tested genes the median I² was 61.2%.

Of the 128 scored genes, 97 were represented in META1 and 26 of these 97 (26.8%) reached FDR < 0.05, against 2,837 of 11,518 non-KpyRN genes (24.6%); a Fisher exact test gave OR = 1.12 (95% exact-conditional CI 0.71–1.77), p = 0.64. So there is no module-level enrichment, and the KpyRN remains a mechanistic hypothesis rather than a statistically enriched gene set. The ISG heterogeneity argues against "global IFN-I suppression". GBP1 and ISG20 rose while IFI44L, IFI27, DHX58, DDX58 and STAT2 fell. That pattern fits a change in the composition of interferon-stimulated transcripts (for example towards NF-κB-driven GBP1) better than uniform blockade. META2 (healer versus non-healer) produced 12 meta-DEGs among 10,300 tested genes, indicating that the healing-outcome contrast carries a much weaker signal than the occurrence contrast, in line with the per-cohort null results.

### 3.2 The three-layer intersection is driven by the pyruvate-supply module, not by the network as a whole

The DFU-correlated modules were magenta (r = −0.79, p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁵), yellow (r = −0.74), red (r = −0.73) and blue (r = +0.70). Their intersection with meta-DEG and KpyRN comprised six genes: BPGM (+0.83, padj 0.026), LDHA (+1.00, 1.1 × 10⁻⁹), PFKP (+0.90, 3.3 × 10⁻⁷), PGAM1 (+1.25, 1.5 × 10⁻³²), PGM2 (+0.67, 2.4 × 10⁻³) and TPI1 (+1.29, 1.4 × 10⁻⁶). The union with the two-layer intersection gave a 26-gene candidate pool.

All six genes belong to KEGG hsa00010, and the pyruvate-supply module was built from that same pathway, so convergence there carries little weight on its own. We therefore pre-specified an independence test rather than reporting the intersection as corroboration (Supplementary Table S11; `03_结果/WGCNA/intersection_independence.json`). Excluding the pyruvate-supply module's 90 genes empties the three-layer intersection: the Kpy machinery (M1), Kpy-target (M3) and IFN-I read-out (M4) modules contribute no members at all. The number of KpyRN genes reaching the DFU-correlated pool is likewise unremarkable. WGCNA was run on 5,000 high-variance genes; 56 of the 128 scored KpyRN genes fall inside that background, and 6 of those 56 entered the pool against 8.9 expected (hypergeometric p = 0.19). The IFN-I module behaves differently: it is depleted rather than neutral. None of its 21 background-covered genes entered the pool (expected 3.3), and 14 of them sit in the turquoise module, which has no relation to DFU status (r = −0.22, p = 0.33). We did not anticipate that depletion. Because the direction of this comparison was not pre-specified, we report the two-sided p (0.052) and the BH-adjusted q across the seven subsets (0.18); this depletion is a directional observation and not a statistically significant result. The permutation arm of the test evaluates only P(X ≥ observed) and therefore cannot corroborate depletion; it is reported for the enrichment direction only. Together these results say that the glycolytic arm co-varies with the DFU transcriptome while the IFN-I arm does not — the opposite of one co-regulated KpyRN. Two caveats carry over: n = 21 is close to the lower limit at which WGCNA is usable, and the soft-threshold fit was unstable here, so we used these modules only as an intersection filter.

### 3.3 A six-gene consensus classifier separates DFU from control in foot skin but does not transfer

Task A (diagnosis) cross-validated AUCs in the discovery set were LASSO 0.983 ± 0.022, random forest 0.969 ± 0.021, SVM-RFE 0.955 ± 0.029 and XGBoost 0.911 ± 0.067. Because all ten seeds re-partition the same 21 samples, these standard deviations describe replicate-to-replicate variability of the cross-validation procedure and are not standard errors of the true performance. In the external GSE199939 cohort, LASSO, random forest and SVM-RFE each achieved AUC 0.991 (95% CI 0.967–1.000), whereas XGBoost achieved 0.500: its selection was dominated by a single gene, FAHD2B, whose expression is nearly invariant in that cohort. Single-algorithm selection failed here: XGBoost built its model on one gene (FAHD2B, importance 0.97) whose expression is nearly invariant in GSE199939. The consensus requirement is what removed that failure mode.

The six-gene consensus logistic-regression model (ADH1B, ALDH3A2, DHX58, FAHD2B, GBP1, LDHA) reached AUC 1.000 in GSE199939. The model separates this 21-sample cohort completely, so the DeLong variance is zero and the interval collapses to 1.000–1.000; the informative quantity is the exact probability of perfect separation under the null, 1/C(21,10) = 2.8 × 10⁻⁶. The consensus gene set and the consensus model were selected and fitted on the full training set, so no internal validation stands behind this number. It should be read as complete separation in this sample — a contrast that is large by construction, since three of the six genes alone separate the cohort almost perfectly — and not as a calibrated performance estimate. All six consensus genes were present in both external cohorts, so no external evaluation was skipped or substituted.

Transfer to forearm skin in GSE143735 was evaluated in two pre-specified contrasts: DFU healers versus non-ulcer diabetic skin (n = 9; LASSO 0.10, random forest 0.35, SVM-RFE 0.20, consensus 0.05) and DFU non-healers versus non-ulcer diabetic skin (n = 8; 0.50, 0.31, 0.81, 0.69). In the first contrast the consensus AUC of 0.05 is significantly below chance (exact one-sided p = 0.016), i.e. the signature is systematically inverted rather than merely uninformative; in the second it is not. The failure to transfer is therefore contrast-dependent, and the model must be restricted to foot-skin material regardless of that heterogeneity. Discrimination only is reported: no calibration statistic (Brier score, calibration slope) is reported, because a six-predictor logistic model fitted on 21 samples is over-fitted by construction and any calibration estimate from it would be uninformative. The consensus model should not be confused with a clinically usable test; it is a device for asking whether the KpyRN genes carry between-group information at all.

Task B (healing outcome) showed no cross-validated signal (AUC 0.39–0.55 across the four algorithms). We therefore do **not** report the external AUCs for this task as results: the single external cohort comprised nine samples, one model reached AUC 1.000 and another 0.850, and with no internal signal to anchor them these values are uninterpretable — they are the spread one expects from a statistic computed on nine observations. They are listed in Supplementary Table S4 for completeness only. The Task B consensus model also uses a different six-gene set (ALDH3A2, DHX58, FAHD2A, FAHD2B, LDHB, MPC2), overlapping the Task A signature in three genes, which is a further reason not to read the two tasks as one signature. We make **no claim** that the KpyRN predicts healing outcome.

### 3.4 Immune deconvolution: a cytotoxic reduction in one cohort, a different phenotype in the other

In GSE134431 (DFS versus DFU, 8 versus 13), six immune metrics differed at FDR < 0.05, all in the same direction — reduced in ulcerated tissue: EPIC CD8 T cells (FDR = 0.0063), MCP-counter CD8 T cells (0.0063), MCP-counter cytotoxic lymphocytes (0.0063), MCP-counter T cells (0.0092), MCP-counter NK cells (0.0101) and MCP-counter myeloid dendritic cells (0.0151). In GSE199939 (DFU versus control, 10 versus 11), six metrics again differed, but in mixed directions: MCP-counter monocytic lineage (4.56 versus 3.14, FDR = 0.0025), MCP-counter fibroblasts (9.59 versus 8.50, 0.0115), EPIC CD4 T cells (0.144 versus 0.111, 0.0115) and EPIC neutrophils (0.154 versus 0.092, 0.0414) were **higher** in DFU, whereas MCP-counter myeloid dendritic cells (1.78 versus 3.55, 0.0268) and EPIC other cells (0.559 versus 0.654, 0.0268) were lower. No cytotoxic or NK metric was significant in GSE199939 (FDR 0.30–0.79). The healer-versus-non-healer comparison in GSE134431 yielded no FDR-significant immune difference.

The two cohorts therefore do **not** converge on a single immune phenotype, and the reduction in cytotoxic infiltrate is a single-cohort finding. It is directionally consistent with the reduced IFN-I read-out of Section 3.1, but that consistency is internal to GSE134431 and is not replication. The two cohorts also do not constitute cross-method corroboration: they were profiled on different platforms, so comparing them tests repeatability, not methods. The genuine cross-method comparison is EPIC versus MCP-counter within a cohort, and that agreement is metric-dependent — high for B cells (Spearman 0.87/0.90) and CD8 T cells (0.91/0.95), lower for neutrophils (0.54/0.93) and negative for NK (−0.40/0.29). The negative EPIC CD4-versus-MCP T-cell correlation is an expected consequence of EPIC's unit-sum constraint and is reported rather than suppressed. Hub-gene–immune correlations were extensive (60/300 significant pairs in GSE134431; 62/300 in GSE199939), the strongest being DHX58 versus MCP-counter CD8 T cells (ρ = +0.840) in GSE134431. No immune-direction claim in this paper rests on more than one cohort.

### 3.5 Single-cell analysis localises the glycolytic shift to keratinocytes

In GSE165816 [11, 12], 174,962 cells passed to 167,837 after QC (95.93%). At donor level in foot skin (14 DFU versus 19 non-DFU), four lineages differed at FDR < 0.05: Mono_CD14 (median proportion 0.0099 versus 0.0010, q = 6.0 × 10⁻³), Mono_CD16 (0.0071 versus 0.0002, q = 6.0 × 10⁻³), Schwann/Neural (0.0005 versus 0.0026, q = 0.017) and NK (0.0047 versus 0.0017, q = 0.037). Forearm skin (5 vs 6), PBMC (5 vs 5), the four-group Kruskal–Wallis test and the healer-versus-non-healer contrast produced no FDR-significant differences.

Pseudobulk analysis of KpyRN genes at donor × lineage level showed that keratinocyte glycolytic enzymes rose together in DFU: PGAM1 (log2 CPM 8.37 vs 7.29), TPI1 (9.26 vs 8.52), LDHA (9.99 vs 9.30), ENO1 (9.85 vs 9.08), PKM (9.86 vs 8.87), GAPDH (11.97 vs 11.27), HK2 (4.12 vs 2.58) and PGM2 (5.51 vs 4.86), all at q < 0.05. This reproduces the bulk meta-analytic direction at single-cell resolution and in a defined cell type. Fibroblasts and pericyte/SMC cells showed reduced ADH1B (2.37 vs 6.57 and 0.67 vs 3.40 respectively) with increased ISG transcripts (IFITM3, ISG20, IFI16). The ISG pattern was again heterogeneous (IFITM3, IFITM1, ISG20, IFI16 and IFIT2 increased; IRF9, JAK1, IFNAR1 and EIF2AK2 decreased), matching the bulk observation: the change is selective, not a shutdown of the whole pathway.

Two method-level caveats were recorded. Hub-gene median values computed over all cells are depressed by the large fraction of zero-expressing cells (PGAM1 detected in 53% of cells, ADH1B in 18%, IFI44L in 5%), so expression patterns must be interpreted from pseudobulk aggregates or from expressing-cell proportions and never from the all-cell median. The two approaches do not agree everywhere. GSE134431 shows fewer lymphocytes in ulcerated tissue, whereas direct single-cell counting shows more monocytes and NK in DFU, and GSE199939 — also deconvolution-based — agrees with the single-cell direction for the monocytic lineage (Section 3.4). The comparator differs (bulk: diabetic non-ulcer foot skin; single-cell: non-diabetic and non-ulcer diabetic skin) and so does the method (deconvolution versus direct counting), so we present both without adjudicating between them.

### 3.6 Two independent validation cohorts provide no positive module-level support

**GSE223964 (diabetic versus non-diabetic skin, 27,998 cells, 8 donors) [15].** We state the design limit first, because it determines what this cohort can say: with five diabetic and three non-diabetic donors, the smallest two-sided Mann–Whitney p attainable for any contrast is 2/C(8,3) = 0.036, so after BH correction across the 32 cell-type × module contrasts the smallest attainable q is 1.0. That all 32 comparisons were non-significant is therefore a property of the design rather than an empirical finding, and we draw no inference from it. A second donor-level path — median instead of mean pseudobulk, 10,000 label permutations, i.e. a different summary statistic and a different reference distribution computed from the same eight donors — returned the same absence of signal. This excludes a computational artefact but is not independent evidence, because both paths use the same donors and the same cells. Directional convergence was nevertheless visible in individual contrasts (Blood Vessels M4 IFN-I read-out higher in diabetic skin, +0.0423 by the mean path and +0.0351 by the permutation path; Keratinocytes M4 +0.0348/+0.0338). The most informative cell-level observation was that **LDHA was detectable in immune cells of diabetic skin (median 1.061 in lymphocytes, 0.914 in macrophages, 1.243 in mast cells) but was 0.000 in the corresponding non-diabetic cells**, whereas fibroblast, keratinocyte and pericyte values were slightly lower in diabetic skin — i.e. LDHA is an immune-cell-restricted elevation in this cohort, not a whole-skin increase. At cell level (which we label descriptive, because cells are nested within donors and this analysis is pseudoreplicated), ALDH3A2-positive cell fraction was lower in diabetic skin (19.0% versus 26.1%), while FAHD2B (3.95% versus 3.24%) and DHX58 (1.51% versus 1.10%) were marginally higher. We stress the limits of this dataset: five versus three donors cannot support module-level inference; five of six hub genes have zero median expression in most cell types; and the matrix is log1p-scaled with no raw counts, so the pseudobulk is not directly comparable with bulk RNA-seq.

**GSE166120 (GeoMx DSP, 23 ROIs from 4 subjects, 2 healers and 2 non-healers).** The CTA v2.0 panel covers only 54 of the 128 scored KpyRN genes (42.2%), with module coverage of M4 IFN-I read-out 26/31 (83.9%), M1 2/4 (50.0%), M3 1/3 (33.3%) and M2 pyruvate supply 25/90 (27.8%); of the six hub genes, only LDHA, ADH1B and GBP1 are on-panel, and FAHD2B, DHX58 and ALDH3A2 are genuinely absent (verified probe by probe). At the subject level (two healers versus two non-healers) no comparison is evaluable: with two subjects per group the smallest attainable two-sided Mann–Whitney p is 2/C(4,2) = 0.33, so the observed p of 1.0 carries no information about the absence of a difference. ROI-level stratification was likewise not testable at subject level, because epidermis was sampled in one subject and non-ulcer tissue in two. A nominal ROI-level p = 0.0405 for the M4 IFN-I read-out by ROI location (ulcer −0.29 versus non-ulcer +0.45) did not survive FDR correction (0.1731) and vanished entirely when recomputed at subject level; the driving value was a single extreme ROI (−1.45) from Subject 3. We classify this as a pseudoreplication-driven false positive and do not report it as a positive finding. Because the panel covers less than half of the scoring set, board-restricted module scores are not comparable to full-KpyRN scores, and this experiment can only provide exploratory regional localisation.

### 3.7 A longitudinal infection cohort shows module-level shifts with no hub-gene signal

In GSE230426 (PBMC, *S. aureus* DFU infection) [14, 16], the whole-transcriptome comparison yielded **zero DEGs at FDR < 0.05 in all three models** (unpaired, inferred-paired, after-8-week-only; 57,059 genes tested). Pre-specified module-level competitive testing was informative: in the primary unpaired model the M4 IFN-I read-out module (31 genes) was down (FDR = 0.0094) and the M3 Kpy-target module (3 genes) was down (FDR = 0.0094), with M2 pyruvate supply nominally down (FDR = 0.061). The inferred-paired sensitivity model gave the same directions (M3 FDR = 0.021; M4 FDR = 0.052), and the after-8-week-only model gave stronger evidence (M4 FDR = 0.0016; M2 FDR = 0.0152; M3 FDR = 0.0159, all down). Concordance between the primary and paired models across the 128 KpyRN genes was high (logFC Spearman r = 0.930; direction agreement 88.3%).

Interpretation is constrained by two facts. First, the direction is *opposite* to that observed in tissue: IFN-I read-out was lower in DFU ulcer tissue relative to non-ulcer skin (Section 3.1) but *higher* in PBMC during active infection than after resolution. These are two different biological processes — local tissue IFN-I suppression versus circulating infection-associated immune activation — and must not be conflated. Second, **all six consensus hub genes were non-significant in PBMC** (|logFC| below 0.52, minimum adjusted p of 0.41, consistent across all three models). This is a substantive negative result: the KpyRN glycolytic hub signal appears tissue-resident rather than circulating, which argues against using blood as a surrogate and argues *for* the tissue-based RT-qPCR protocol of Section 2.12.

### 3.8 Mendelian randomisation: one FDR-significant estimate, at DHX58

Under the two-sample MR framework [29], instruments were cis-eQTL lead variants from eQTLGen (up to 31,684 whole-blood donors) and outcomes came from three public GWAS (Supplementary Table S8b). Thirteen of fifteen candidate genes had a significant cis-eQTL lead instrument (ADH1B and PKM had none and were recorded as SKIPPED), with F statistics from 51 to 2,186, all above the F > 10 threshold. Across 34 gene–outcome pairs with BH-FDR correction, exactly one pair was significant: **DHX58 → type 2 diabetes**, Wald β = −0.0712 (SE 0.0195; 95% CI −0.109 to −0.033), p = 2.6 × 10⁻⁴, FDR = 0.0088, OR = 0.931 (95% CI 0.896–0.968) per unit of eQTL-driven expression, instrument F = 1,189. The direction was concordant in a second, partially overlapping T2D dataset (β = −0.0446, nominal p = 0.0081, FDR = 0.138). Four additional gene–outcome pairs were nominally significant (p < 0.05) but did not survive correction: PGAM1 → T2D in only one of the two T2D sources (β = −0.0266, p = 0.040; the other source was non-significant, p = 0.11), LDHA → fasting insulin (β = +0.0269, p = 0.023), SIRT3 → fasting insulin (β = −0.0261, p = 0.032) and DHX58 → fasting insulin (β = −0.0104, p = 0.046). We read DHX58 → T2D as supportive but weak. Higher DHX58 expression associates with lower T2D risk, whereas lower DHX58 accompanies an IFN-I-suppressed, infection-susceptible tissue state. One FDR-significant pair among 34 tests does not establish a causal mechanism.

### 3.9 Docking screens five target proteins but no candidate outperforms the co-crystal reference

Re-docking reproduced the crystallographic pose in all eight systems (heavy-atom RMSD below 0.001 Å), within the pre-set < 2 Å criterion. The systems were: PKM2 C-pocket, PKM2 A-pocket, EP300 acetyl-CoA site, SIRT3 ADP-ribose/NAD⁺ pocket, LDHA active site, PDK2 ATP pocket, PDK2 pyruvate site, PDK2 R-domain N-terminal site. This result is reported with its limitation. The co-crystallographic ligands were prepared as rigid molecules (zero rotatable bonds) and docked from their own crystal conformation in a box centred on their centroid, so pose recovery is close to tautological and validates box placement and receptor preparation only. It does not validate the torsional search applied to the flexible candidates (11–15 rotatable bonds), and because the rigid references carry no intramolecular strain term while the flexible candidates do, the Δ values below are biased in favour of the co-crystal reference. Across the 55 docking runs (29 unique candidates, 8 positive controls, 1 negative control per system) the most favourable candidates were LDHA CHEMBL4782843 (−10.35 kcal/mol; co-crystal −13.16, Δ = +2.81) and CHEMBL4859303 (−10.29, Δ = +2.87); PDK2 ATP-pocket CHEMBL6011956 (−8.01; co-crystal −9.58, Δ = +1.57); PDK2 R-domain CHEMBL5896437 (−9.06; co-crystal −11.39, Δ = +2.33); PKM2 A-pocket CHEMBL5590652 (−8.92; co-crystal −12.22, Δ = +3.30); EP300 CHEMBL6021203 (−10.79; co-crystal −17.10, Δ = +6.31); SIRT3 CHEMBL2332041 (−11.50; co-crystal −21.26, Δ = +9.76); and PKM2 C-pocket TEPP-46 (−10.25; co-crystal 1OX −20.30, Δ = +10.05). Literature reference ligands behaved as expected: FX11 (−7.98) and galloflavin (−7.88) for LDHA, A-485 (−10.58) and C646 (−10.17) for EP300, quercetin (−9.61) and sirtinol (−8.70) for SIRT3, and oxamic acid (−4.07) weak in line with its known weak inhibition.

The negative control (sucrose) was among the weakest binders in every system (affinities from −4.01 to −7.45 kcal/mol across the eight systems), indicating no non-specific false-positive signal. One candidate scored nominally better than its co-crystal (CHEMBL5777543 at the PDK2 pyruvate site, −4.82 versus −4.08, Δ = −0.73); however, in that same shallow pocket sucrose (−4.01) was indistinguishable from the co-crystal, the pocket has a low depth (Δ ≈ −0.73 kcal/mol against a 22 × 22 × 22 Å box) and the discrimination of the protocol is inadequate there. We therefore **degrade this observation** and do not claim superiority over the co-crystal. Similarly, the PKM2 C-pocket system is dominated by a 42-heavy-atom co-crystal whose absolute score is unusually strong, so that system is interpreted through the A-pocket cross-check rather than its absolute value. Three ChEMBL candidates with functional but site-unannotated activity were docked in all three PDK2 pockets, and the site uncertainty is reported explicitly. All candidates are described as requiring experimental validation (SPR/ITC/enzyme activity/cellular assays).

One further caveat governs the Δ comparison, and it changes the conclusion. AutoDock Vina scores improve monotonically with molecular size, so Δ = candidate − co-crystal measures size difference as much as binding. Normalising by heavy-atom count (ligand efficiency, Supplementary Table S9c) reverses the ordering in five of the eight systems — PKM2 C-pocket (−0.483 for 1OX versus −0.517 for CHEMBL5573729), EP300 (−0.335 for acetyl-CoA versus −0.340 for curcumin), SIRT3 (−0.591 for AR6 versus −0.695 for nicotinamide), LDHA (−0.399 for ZHK versus −0.678 for oxamic acid) and the PDK2 pyruvate site (−0.680 for dichloroacetate versus −0.704 for pyruvate). The two metrics disagree because each is biased in a known direction: raw affinity favours large molecules, ligand efficiency favours small ones. No uncertainty estimate is available, because each system was run once at exhaustiveness 8 with a single seed, whereas Vina's typical scoring error is 2–3 kcal/mol — a range that brackets several of the Δ values reported above. The statement that no candidate outperformed its crystallographic reference is therefore a statement about this protocol at this exhaustiveness, and it should not be read as evidence about which molecule binds better.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 What the data support

The KpyRN transcriptomic footprint shifts in diabetic foot ulcer tissue, and the shift is directional rather than uniform: glycolytic enzyme abundance rises, while only part of the IFN-I read-out falls. Two cross-platform findings support the glycolytic arm: bulk meta-analysis across two cohorts showed 26/26 direction concordance, and single-cell pseudobulk localised the same shift to keratinocytes. A third, homologous observation — the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier MPC2 fell — connects cytosolic pyruvate accumulation to the writer substrate pool, but it reflects the same glycolytic-axis signal rather than an independent line of evidence. The immune signal points the same way in only one of the two bulk cohorts: GSE134431 places reduced cytotoxic infiltration in the same direction as the reduced IFN-I read-out, which is the functional axis that Kpy was shown to influence in [2]. GSE199939 does not reproduce this, so the alignment rests on a single cohort and is stated as such.

Our data are consistent with the model proposed by Zuo et al. — increased glycolytic flux raising pyruvate availability for the Kpy machinery, with downstream attenuation of IFN-I signalling and antimicrobial immunity — but they are equally consistent with a simpler model in which wound hypoxia and hyperglycaemia independently raise glycolysis and independently suppress IFN-I responsiveness, with no Kpy involvement at all. Distinguishing these requires measuring pyruvylation itself, which we did not do. We have no antibody-based or mass-spectrometric measurement of Kpy, and our entire analysis concerns the *transcript* of the network, not the modification. A reader should treat "KpyRN dysregulation" as shorthand for "transcriptional evidence consistent with altered pyruvate availability and a selective IFN-I defect".

The three-layer intersection looks stronger than it is. The pyruvate-supply module is constructed from KEGG glycolysis and pyruvate-metabolism genes, so any intersection with a DFU-correlated co-expression pool will preferentially return glycolytic enzymes; that the six returned genes are all glycolytic is therefore close to a tautology, and we report it as such rather than as a convergence of independent screens. The independence test in Section 3.2 goes further: the Kpy machinery, Kpy-target and IFN-I modules contribute nothing to the intersection, the number of KpyRN genes reaching the pool is indistinguishable from random, and the IFN-I module is under-represented in it (0 of 21 against 3.3 expected; two-sided p = 0.052, BH q = 0.18 across seven subsets — a directional observation, not a significant result). The constructive consequence is therefore narrower than we would like. The data are compatible with **two partially independent axes** — a glycolytic arm that co-varies with the DFU transcriptome and an IFN-I arm that does not — but they do not establish that structure, and the depletion statistic on which it partly rests does not survive correction. We carry it forward as a hypothesis to be tested by the paired design in Supplementary Material S4b, not as a finding.

### 4.2 What the validation cohorts constrain

Two of the four validation cohorts produced no positive module-level signal (GSE223964 at donor level; GSE166120 at subject level) and the third (GSE230426) produced module-level but not gene-level signal, in a different compartment. These attempts did not merely fail to replicate; each one narrows what we can claim.

**Where the biology sits.** The KpyRN glycolytic hub signal was found in *ulcer tissue* and in *keratinocytes of ulcer tissue*, and was absent from PBMC in both the infection cohort and the single-cell cohort. Tissue restriction rules out circulating surrogates, and it is why the pre-specified validation protocol specifies tissue rather than blood.

**How much power each layer has.** Detectability varies by layer. Bulk meta-analysis (11,615–17,239 genes per cohort, 21 and 21 samples) resolves KpyRN modules; the 8-donor single-cell cohort and the 4-subject spatial cohort cannot, because module scores computed from 3–5 donors per group have almost no power against a small effect. Those negative results are therefore best read as *underpowered with respect to the effect size observed in bulk*: the samples needed for the cell-type-specific and region-specific versions of these questions exceed anything currently public.

**Why each cohort failed.** Each negative result has an identifiable cause. The GeoMx experiment could cover only 42.2% of the scoring set and 3 of 6 hub genes, so even a large true effect in the uncovered two-thirds of the network would have been invisible by design. The single-cell dataset lacked raw counts, which caps pseudobulk fidelity. The infection cohort lacked donor identifiers and healing-outcome metadata, which forced an unpaired primary model and precluded the outcome contrast. Reporting these simply as "negative results" would misrepresent them; reporting them without their structural causes would understate the evidence.

### 4.3 Reading the IFN-I heterogeneity

Both the bulk meta-analysis and the single-cell pseudobulk show a mixed ISG picture: IFI44L, IFI27, DHX58, DDX58 and STAT2 fall, while ISG20 and GBP1 rise, and in single cells IFITM3, IFITM1, ISG20, IFI16 and IFIT2 rise while IRF9, JAK1, IFNAR1 and EIF2AK2 fall. A uniform IFN-I blockade cannot produce this pattern. The most economical interpretation is a change in the *composition* of the interferon-stimulated transcriptome — for instance, a shift from interferon-stimulated genes whose transcription depends on IRF9/STAT2 complexes towards those driven by inflammatory NF-κB or interferon-γ — rather than a change in the total strength of interferon signalling. Practically, a single ISG would be a poor read-out of the Kpy axis. The informative tissue measurement is the paired canonical type-I ISG versus inflammatory ISG contrast.

The immune phenotype does not tell that story in both cohorts. GSE134431 shows reduced cytotoxic T-cell and NK infiltrate in ulcerated tissue; GSE199939 shows no significant change in any cytotoxic metric and an increase in monocytic-lineage scores. The apparent tension between reduced monocytic infiltrate in GSE134431 and increased monocytic *proportion* in the single-cell donor-level composition analysis is therefore a single-cohort observation rather than a cross-cohort discrepancy, and it most likely reflects a change in the composition of the infiltrate (fewer infiltrating lymphocytes per unit of monocytes, or a shift towards a myeloid-dominated infiltrate) rather than an absolute loss of monocytes. We report it without resolving it.

### 4.4 How far the classifier travels

The external AUC of 1.000 for the six-gene consensus model is impressive on its face and should not be read as a clinical performance estimate. It comes from 21 samples (10 DFU versus 11 control), the confidence interval collapses to the boundary because the model separates the sample perfectly, and the same model scores 0.05 on forearm skin in one of two pre-specified contrasts — significantly below chance (exact one-sided p = 0.016), so the signature is inverted there rather than merely uninformative, while in the second contrast it scores 0.69. Why XGBoost failed matters more than the fact that it did: a single-gene-dominated model (FAHD2B, importance 0.97) that yields exactly chance externally (AUC 0.500) shows how easily a high in-sample cross-validated AUC (0.911 ± 0.067) can coexist with zero external signal. Had we reported the best single algorithm, we would have reported chance performance with a confident-looking confidence interval. This is why the four-algorithm consensus, the 10-seed repetition, the external cohort and the honest reporting of the forearm-skin failure are reported together.

The six-gene signature is ADH1B, ALDH3A2, FAHD2B, DHX58, GBP1 and LDHA, but only LDHA and GBP1 are canonical M2/M4 members; ADH1B, ALDH3A2 and FAHD2B are metabolic genes whose tissue effects are large but heterogeneous (I² above 87% for all three). ADH1B is reduced by −4.42 log2FC in tissue, and the pooled effect sizes that motivated the original RT-qPCR sample-size calculation are unstable for these genes — their assays were in any case disqualified by the final BLAST pass (Section 2.12). Their failure to replicate in tissue would not falsify the core glycolytic result, and their replication would be strong positive evidence.

### 4.5 Causal inference: one signal, appropriately hedged

The DHX58 → type 2 diabetes result (OR 0.931 per unit increase in cis-eQTL-driven DHX58 expression) is directionally interesting: the KpyRN's reduced IFN-I read-out in DFU tissue includes lower DHX58, and DHX58 expression is inversely associated with T2D risk, i.e. higher DHX58 is protective. A model in which reduced DHX58 marks a tissue state permissive to infection and whose expression is causally related to metabolic disease is coherent with the framework. But one FDR-significant pair out of 34 tests, with all three nominally significant metabolic pairs failing correction, is weak evidence. We also note that DHX58 and DDX58 are alternative names for RIG-I; in one ENSG-to-symbol mapping used in the infection-cohort pipeline DDX58 appeared "missing" solely because the mapping recorded the alias RIGI, and it was recovered by alias normalisation — a reminder that apparent gene-level absences in public data are frequently nomenclature artefacts.

### 4.6 What docking can and cannot show

Passing the positive-control criterion in all eight systems establishes that box placement and receptor preparation are sound; because the controls are rigid and were docked from their own crystal conformation, it does not establish that the protocol recovers poses for flexible drug-like ligands, and it says nothing about whether any candidate binds. Every candidate in every system scored weaker than its co-crystallographic reference on raw affinity, which is the expected outcome for a screen of small molecules against deep, well-defined pockets that were optimised by evolution for a large cofactor — and, as Section 3.9 notes, that ordering reverses in five of eight systems once scores are normalised by heavy-atom count, so even this negative conclusion is protocol-dependent rather than chemical. The practical conclusion is not that no compound binds, but that ranking these candidates requires biophysical validation. The one apparently superior result (PDK2 pyruvate site, Δ = −0.73) is instructive for a different reason: in that shallow pocket the sucrose negative control was indistinguishable from the co-crystal (−4.01 versus −4.08), which shows that a numerical "win" in a poorly discriminating system is uninformative. Reporting that number without the sucrose comparison would have overstated a result that the negative control contradicts.

### 4.7 Limitations

The constraints below apply to the whole analysis. (1) Kpy was not measured; all findings concern the transcriptional footprint of the network.

**Data availability and sample size.** (2) Two bulk cohorts (GSE80178, GSE68183/86) could not be processed at sample level because the pdInfo package was unavailable, and were therefore not used for computation; this reduced the effective meta-analytic breadth. (3) The discovery cohorts are small (21 samples each), and WGCNA at n = 21 with an unstable soft-threshold fit is at the lower limit of validity; module results were used only as a filter. (4) The infection cohort lacks explicit donor identifiers and healing-outcome metadata. (5) GeoMx coverage is partial (42.2% of the scoring set) and provides no upstream normalisation metrics. (6) The single-cell annotation is marker-score based, with 12.7% ambiguous cells and only 1–2 markers for several sub-lineages, so sub-lineage claims are exploratory.

**Level of inference and design limits.** (7) The GeoMx comparison is nested (23 ROIs in 4 subjects) and the single-cell comparisons are nested (cells in 8 donors); we enforced donor- and subject-level inference as primary throughout, and we classify a nominal ROI-level result that disappears on re-analysis at the correct level as a false positive rather than a finding. (8) The RT-qPCR validation protocol was pre-specified but **not executed, and no execution is planned**; it is presented as a design record, and the manuscript's claims are correspondingly limited to public-data inference. Relatedly, the healing-outcome arm of the question remains **unanswered rather than unanswerable**: exactly one public dataset provides a healed-versus-unhealed stratification of human DFU tissue — GSE166120 (2 healers versus 2 non-healers, 12-week outcome) — and it was analysed here and was null at subject level (Section 3.6); the two bulk per-cohort healed-versus-unhealed comparisons yielded zero DEGs and their meta-analysis only 12 meta-DEGs. With n = 4 subjects and a panel covering 42.2% of the scoring set, that dataset cannot resolve the outcome question, and closing the arm would require prospective tissue sampling with 12-week follow-up, which the design record specifies (Supplementary Material S4b) but which this work does not attempt; the outcome gap therefore remains open rather than merely unexecuted. (9) The LDHA RT-qPCR assay is anchored to a 3'UTR segment of NM_005566.4 that is shared by the six LDHA RefSeq transcripts recovered as full-length BLAST targets; it therefore reports aggregate LDHA transcript abundance rather than a single isoform, and the read-out should not be interpreted as isoform-specific. The design deliberately excludes the processed pseudogene LOC105375980, which shares 96.16% of the LDHA coding sequence and would co-amplify under any CDS-anchored design; the resulting coverage is therefore LDHA transcripts that carry this 3'UTR segment, not every LDHA-related locus in the genome. (10) Bulk transcriptome analysis cannot separate a change in per-cell expression from a change in cell-type composition, and the two bulk cohorts disagree about the direction of the immune infiltrate (Section 3.4) while deconvolution and direct single-cell counting disagree about monocytes; no cell-composition adjustment was applied to the meta-analysis, so part of the reported differential expression may reflect composition. (11) The cohorts include no non-diabetic wound control, so we cannot distinguish changes that are specific to diabetic ulceration from those common to any chronic wound. (12) Throughout, a null p is reported as *not evaluable* rather than as evidence of no difference wherever the design cannot produce a small p: with two subjects per group the minimum attainable two-sided Mann–Whitney p is 0.33, and with five versus three donors it is 0.036 (Section 3.6). Two further constraints are stated where they arise rather than repeated here: the single-instrument MR design cannot test its own assumptions (Section 2.10), and the docking comparison is protocol-dependent in both directions (Section 3.9).

### 4.8 What would move this forward

Four experiments would test what this study cannot. (i) Direct quantification of Kpy in DFU versus non-ulcer tissue by mass spectrometry or a validated antibody, ideally with the paired pyruvate measurement already specified in the protocol, so that the modification itself becomes testable, not only its transcript. (ii) Execution of the pre-specified tissue RT-qPCR with the paired ISG-versus-metabolic-gene contrast, which would test the two arms of the dysregulation separately rather than as a composite. (iii) A larger donor-level single-cell or spatial DFU cohort with full panel coverage, sufficient to test the cell-type-restricted LDHA pattern we observed at n = 5 versus 3. (iv) A bisulfite or CUT&Tag-based analysis of the Kpy-target promoters (FGFR4, LGALS8, RSPH4A) to test the epigenetic arm of the mechanism directly.

## 5. Conclusions

The lysine-pyruvylation regulatory network is transcriptionally altered in diabetic foot ulcer tissue: glycolytic enzyme expression rises reproducibly, the type-I interferon read-out is attenuated selectively rather than uniformly, and cytotoxic infiltration is reduced in one of the two bulk cohorts. The KpyRN is not enriched among meta-DEGs as a set (26 of 97 genes versus a 24.6% background rate; Fisher OR 1.12, p = 0.64), so what reproduces here is the behaviour of specific genes — the glycolytic core and a subset of interferon-stimulated transcripts — not the network as a statistical unit. On public data alone this is where the KpyRN can be taken; direct measurement of Kpy in tissue is the step that would move it beyond inference. One causal estimate survived correction (DHX58 → type 2 diabetes, OR 0.931, 95% CI 0.896–0.968) on a single instrument, so its assumptions cannot be tested. Docking covered five target proteins in eight pockets with a protocol whose positive controls are rigid, and no candidate outscored its crystallographic reference on raw affinity — an ordering that reverses in five of eight systems once scores are normalised by molecular size. Kpy itself was not measured, and the tissue RT-qPCR protocol specified here was not executed and is not planned. We position the KpyRN as a candidate metabolic-state readout of the DFU microenvironment whose evidentiary boundary this study defines, rather than as an established mechanism.
